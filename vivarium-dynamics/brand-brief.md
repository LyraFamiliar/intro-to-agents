# Brand brief — decisions and rationale

Every choice below came out of a consultant-style interview. The *why* matters
more than the *what*, so it's recorded alongside each decision.

## Round 1 — constraints

| Question | Decision | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Where to sit on the Sea Monkeys credibility gap (gorgeous ad art vs. brine shrimp reality) | **Earnestly overpromise** — play it straight, no wink | The gap *is* the brand memory. Sincerity is funnier and more magical than self-awareness, but the product has to survive the letdown |
| Who pays, who opens the box | **Both, deliberately** — kid retail line + collector line under one identity | Forces one identity with two vocabularies |
| Do the creatures die, and does the brand admit it | **Lifespan as a feature** — a "season" | Creates ritual, stakes, and re-purchase. Also the single biggest liability |
| In-world 90s brand, or modern brand using 90s codes | **Modern brand, 90s costume** | Commercially flexible |

### Conflict 1 — sincerity vs. costume

Earnest overpromise only works if the brand believes itself. "Modern brand in
90s costume" tells the audience you're both in on it. Combined carelessly you
get parody, which kills the magic.

**Resolution:** the *brand* is sincere; the era cues are merely its house style.
The copy never winks. Codified as prohibition P-04, "No irony."

### Conflict 2 — children and mortality

Telling a 7-year-old their pet has a "season" is a parent-complaint engine.
Great for collectors, dangerous at retail.

**Resolution:** two vocabularies. Specimens™ (retail) frames the lifecycle as
sleep and eggs and never mentions Class. The Vivarium Registry (collector)
carries the full clinical record.

## Round 2 — the product

| Question | Decision | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Core fantasy of the creature | **Each one is unique** — genetic lottery, randomized traits | Strongest commercial engine: discovery and collecting |
| What the brand tells a child about the end of a season | **Dormancy, not death** — plus a paid preservation service | The joke that became the business model |
| Naming territory | **Fake-corporate biotech** | Comedy from deadpan authority; fits earnest overpromise |

**The taxidermy upsell was an offhand joke and it became the thesis.** A company
that sells you a unique living creature, a guaranteed ending, and then sells you
the ending again as furniture.

## Round 3 — the grilling

| Question | Decision | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| You've built a loot box aimed at minors. Now what? | **Rarity is discovered, not sold** — every egg the same price, no published odds, no premium packs | Keeps the collecting magic, defuses the gambling charge. Codified as P-02 |
| Toy brand or satire? | **Sincere toy, dark if you look** | Surface reads delightful; the horror is only in the fine print |
| What does the company refuse to do? | **No wild release** | Inverts beautifully — it's not protecting the world from Specimens, it's protecting Specimens from *nature*. Containment as ideology, not safety. P-01 |
| Who is the enemy? | **Nature / chance** | This is the keystone. It reframes everything below |

### What the keystone fixed

- **The Registry became a casualty log, not a rarity market.** They're
  documenting failures with institutional thoroughness. "No two have ever been
  alike" is simultaneously marketing and confession.
- **The tone problem resolved.** The dark thing isn't cruelty, it's hubris —
  which is sympathetic, and works on both audiences at once.
- **The tagline became a lie the company believes.** "Life, individually
  authored" — they didn't author it. Chance did.

## Round 4 — the world

| Question | Decision | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Does the company know its failures are its greatest hits? | **Actively suppressed** — anomalies recovered, "offered complimentary Preservation" | Generates a collector black market |
| What does an egg cost? | **$4.99, deliberately trivial** | The creature is nearly free and the grief is expensive. Sharpest statement of the economics |
| Founders | **Two who split** — one wanted control, one wanted anomaly | Gives the company a central conflict and a built-in rival |

### Reconciliation — suppression as love

"Actively suppressed" and "sincere toy" don't fit *unless the suppression is
love.* The Voluntary Specimen Recovery Program™ offers complimentary
Preservation and a $200 Registry credit for any Specimen "exhibiting deviation
from intended expression." Not a recall — a rescue.

Kessler genuinely believes an Anomalous Specimen is suffering: that it was
failed at the moment of authorship, and that letting it live in a child's
bedroom is cruelty. **She is wrong. The anomalies are thriving.** That's the
horror, and it's entirely sympathetic.

### Unresolved: the economic loop

A $4.99 egg with a $200 buy-back means an anomaly is worth 40 eggs. Collectors
will farm cases hunting deviations, and the smart ones won't report them — which
rebuilds the loot box that P-02 defused, through the side door. Fen Biologic runs
an unofficial adoption network for exactly these.

**Not a bug — the best story in the brand.** Vivarium's war on chance funds a
black market in chance. Open question: does the company know?

## Cast

**Dr. Miriam Kessler** — wanted control. Stayed. Writes the manuals, the fine
print, the dormancy language. Every sincere, adoring, faintly chilling sentence
in this brand is hers.

**Dr. Aleksander Fen** — wanted anomaly. Left in 1997. Founded **Fen Biologic**,
tagline *"Let them be strange."* Vivarium has never named him in print; he
appears only as "a former member of the founding team." That omission is the
loudest thing on the box.
